Where are Britain's oldest trees?

Some of Britain's trees were already old when the Normans arrived. I mapped every notable tree that volunteers have put on OpenStreetMap, {{counts.notable|int}} of them, to ask where the big old ones stand, which species they are, why so many ancient yews are in churchyards, and how much a tree's girth can tell you about its age.

In the churchyard at Fortingall in Perthshire stands a yew that Forestry and Land Scotland reckon is {{fam.Fortingall Yew.age_hi|int}} years old. Other estimates are a more modest {{fam.Fortingall Yew.age_lo|int}} to 3,000 years. Nobody really knows, because the heart of a hollow yew has long since rotted away and with it the rings you would need to count. When its girth was measured in 1771 it was 56 feet round, about {{fam.Fortingall Yew.girth_m|f1}} metres.

Britain is unusually rich in very old trees, partly because so much of its landscape was kept as deer park, royal forest, wood pasture and churchyard for centuries. But where exactly are they? The best inventory, the Woodland Trust's Ancient Tree Inventory, is not open for reuse, so I have not used it. Instead I asked what the open data know: OpenStreetMap, the volunteer map of the world, plus Wikidata and Wikipedia. The answer is part natural history and part portrait of the people who map trees.

Read this first: these are mapped trees, not all trees

OpenStreetMap shows trees where volunteers have chosen to map them. A county with hundreds of dots may simply have one keen mapper; a county with none may be full of ancient oaks. The Woodland Trust's Ancient Tree Inventory holds far more records, but its data are not openly licensed for reuse, so this study does not use or reproduce them. Every pattern below is about the {{counts.notable|int}} trees in the open map, and I say how far each one can be trusted.

      02 · Mappers, not treesA map of enthusiasm

      My extract of OpenStreetMap holds {{counts.osm_trees_extract|int}} trees in the United Kingdom with a name, girth, age or similar tag. Almost all of them, {{counts.extract_age_class|int}}, are street and park trees imported from council surveys with an age class such as "mature" or "young". Only {{counts.notable|int}} pass my filter for a notable tree. Of these, {{counts.named|int}} have a name, {{counts.natural_monument|int}} are tagged as a natural monument, {{counts.landmark|int}} as a landmark and {{counts.girth3|int}} have a credible girth of 3 m or more.

      The pattern is lumpy in a way that real trees are not. {{grid.top10_share|pct0}} of all the notable trees fall in just ten 10 km squares of the National Grid, and the busiest single square, around {{grid.top1_place|raw}} near Barnsley, holds {{grid.top1_n|int}}. That is not a Yorkshire forest of giants: {{habits.top.0.n|int}} unnamed trees of unrecorded species in Barnsley are tagged as "landmarks", as are {{habits.top.1.n|int}} in Northumberland. In Coventry, {{habits.top.4.n|int}} unnamed trees carry the natural monument tag. Across Britain, {{habits.n|int}} notable trees ({{habits.share|pct0}}) are unnamed trees of unknown species flagged in this way: the habits of a handful of mappers.

      Wales, home to some of Britain's most famous yews, has just {{country.Wales.n|int}} notable trees on the open map; Scotland has {{country.Scotland.n|int}} and Northern Ireland {{country.Northern Ireland.n|int}}, against {{country.England.n|int}} in England. And even the stars are patchy: of the {{wikidata.n|int}} trees in Wikidata described as remarkable or heritage trees in the UK, I could find {{wikidata.found|int}} ({{wikidata.share|pct0}}) on OpenStreetMap within 150 m of the Wikidata point. The famous Llangernyw Yew in Conwy, which Wikipedia says may be over 4,000 years old, does not appear as a named tree in my extract at all.

      The open map of old trees is a map of enthusiasm: it shows where people have gone out to look.

      03 · Which treesMostly oaks

      Only {{counts.species_known|int}} of the notable trees ({{counts.species_known_share|pct0}}) have a species, either tagged or plain from their name ("the Wortley Oak"). Among those, the oak dominates: {{sp.oak.n|int}} trees, {{sp.oak.share|pct0}} of the known total, and {{sp.oak.old_share|pct0}} of the known-species trees with a sign of great age.

      Species of notable trees, where known

      Species from the species, taxon or genus tag, or failing that from the tree's name. Sycamore and maples are counted together, as are birch, alder, willow and poplar.

      Oaks are the classic old tree of English parks and hedgerows, and they include the biggest girths on the map: the Queen Elizabeth Oak near Lodsworth in West Sussex is tagged at {{big.Queen Elizabeth Oak.girth|f2}} m round, and Majesty, the Fredville Oak in Kent, at {{big.Majesty - the Fredville Oak.girth|f2}} m. Next to them stands a sweet chestnut, the Cowdray Colossus, at {{big.'Cowdray Colossus' Chestnut.girth|f1}} m. The second most common group of old-looking trees is a surprise: {{sp.giant redwoods.n|int}} giant redwoods, {{sp.giant redwoods.old|int}} of them with a girth of 3 m or more or a monument tag. They are big, but they are newcomers from California, planted in parks and estates from the nineteenth century onwards; a large girth is not the same thing as great age.

      04 · Churchyard yewsThe trees by the church door

      Yews are different. Of the {{church.yew_n|int}} yews anywhere in my OpenStreetMap extract, {{church.yew_within|int}} ({{church.yew_share|pct0}}) stand within {{church.m|int}} m of a mapped church or chapel. For every other kind of tree the figure is {{church.other_share|pct1}}. A yew is {{church.ratio|f1}} times as likely as other trees to be in or by a churchyard. Among the notable yews the link is stronger still: {{church.notable_yew_within|int}} of {{church.notable_yew_n|int}} ({{church.notable_yew_share|pct0}}).

      The churchyard yews belong to the old parish churches, not to the chapels. Of the {{church.yew_within|int}} yews by a church, {{church.yew_kind.anglican or catholic|int}} are nearest an Anglican, Church in Wales or Catholic church and only {{church.yew_kind.nonconformist or other|int}} nearest a Methodist, Baptist or other chapel, although chapels make up {{church.worship_kind.nonconformist or other|int}} of the {{counts.worship|int}} places of worship on the map. Why yews and churches go together is an old argument: shelter, symbolism, a pre-Christian sacred site taken over by the church. These data cannot settle it. But they do show that, on the open map at least, old yews are found where the ground has been a churchyard for a very long time.

      Five of the six famous ancient yews I list from Wikipedia (Fortingall, Llangernyw, Crowhurst, Ashbrittle and Defynnog) are in churchyards. Wales stands out, if only faintly in the open data: {{church_country.Wales.with_yew|int}} of its {{church_country.Wales.churches|int}} mapped places of worship have a mapped yew within {{church.m|int}} m, {{church_country.Wales.per1000|f1}} per thousand, against {{church_country.England.per1000|f1}} per thousand in England. These are tiny numbers, and they say more about how few yews are mapped than about how many grow.

      05 · Royal forestsHunting grounds and their giants

      After 1066 the Norman kings put great tracts of land under forest law to protect deer and the "vert", the greenery that fed them. At the height of the system, Wikipedia notes, a third of southern England was royal forest. A forest was a legal zone, not necessarily a wood, but where land stayed as wood pasture and park for centuries, trees could grow old. Wikidata gives a point for {{forest.n_forests|int}} former royal forests in Britain. Circles of {{forest.km|int}} km round those points cover {{forest.land_share|pct1}} of the land.

      Near a former royal forest: share of land and share of trees
      Within {{forest.km|int}} km of a Wikidata royal forest point.

      Those circles hold {{forest.notable_share|pct1}} of the notable trees and {{forest.old_share|pct1}} of the trees with a sign of great age, about {{forest.old_ratio_land|f1}} times their share of the land. That is {{forest.old_in|int}} trees, so it is a hint rather than a finding, and {{fo_top2_old|int}} of the {{forest.old_in|int}} are in just two places. In Savernake Forest in Wiltshire, a royal forest that is still a forest, three mapped oaks are among the biggest girths on the open map: the Big Belly Oak ({{big.Big Belly Oak.girth|f2}} m), the King of Limbs ({{big.King of Limbs.girth|f1}} m) and the Cathedral Oak ({{big.Cathedral Oak.girth|f1}} m). In the New Forest stand the Knightwood Oak and a group of giant redwoods. The Major Oak in Sherwood Forest, which Wikipedia reports was found to have died in 2026, is on the map too.

      Deer parks, the private hunting enclosures of medieval lords, should be just as rich in old trees. But only {{counts.deer_park_polygons|int}} areas named as deer parks are drawn in OpenStreetMap, covering {{counts.deer_park_km2|f1}} km², and just {{counts.deer_park_trees|int}} notable trees fall inside them. That is a gap in the map, not in the landscape.

      06 · How old?What girth can and cannot tell you

      The only safe way to age a tree is to count its rings, and the oldest trees are usually hollow. Foresters use girth instead. Alan Mitchell's rule of thumb is that a tree with a full crown, growing in the open, adds about {{girth.mitchell_cm|f1}} cm (an inch) of girth a year, measured at 1.5 m above the ground. By that rule, a girth of 3 m means about {{girth.y3|int}} years, 5 m about {{girth.y5|int}} and 10 m about {{girth.y10|int}}. Wikipedia's article on veteran trees suggests 3 m at 1.5 m as one rough test for a veteran.

      Credible girths of notable trees, with Mitchell's rough age
      {{girth.ge3|int}} notable trees with a credible girth of 3 m or more. Darker part of each bar: oaks.
      A girth is credible here if it parses to between 0.1 and 16.5 m, is not one of a block of ten or more identical values in the same square kilometre (survey defaults), is not a bare whole number on a tree with no other tags, and, above 9 m, belongs to a named or monument-tagged tree. Unitless numbers above 25 are read as centimetres.

      Only {{girth.n|int}} notable trees carry a girth I trust, and {{girth.ge10|int}} of them measure 10 m or more. The rule of thumb is a good start for a tree a few centuries old. For the truly ancient it fails badly, because old trees slow down, lose their crowns and hollow out. Set the girths Wikipedia gives for famous trees against the ages it reports and the gap is enormous.

      Mitchell's rule against the ages claimed for famous trees
      Age in years. Dot: age from girth at {{girth.mitchell_cm|f1}} cm a year. Bar: the range of ages reported on Wikipedia.
      Girths and ages as stated in the English Wikipedia articles (retrieved {{retrieved|raw}}); the girths were measured at different heights and dates, so the dots are rough. The Ashbrittle and Defynnog articles give no girth figure, and the Bowthorpe Oak article gives no age.

      By Mitchell's rule the Llangernyw Yew, {{fam.Llangernyw Yew.girth_m|f2}} m round at ground level, would be about {{fam.Llangernyw Yew.mitchell_years|int}} years old; the churchyard certificate says 4,000 to 5,000. The Major Oak, {{fam.Major Oak.girth_m|f2}} m round in 1999, comes out at about {{fam.Major Oak.mitchell_years|int}} years, against the 800 to 1,200 usually quoted. A big girth tells you a tree is old. It cannot tell you how old, and for yews it barely tells you anything beyond "very".

      07 · LimitsWhat this can and cannot show

      The map shows mappers. Nearly everything here depends on who has mapped trees and how they tagged them. Counts by region measure enthusiasm as much as trees. The Woodland Trust's Ancient Tree Inventory and the Tree Register hold far richer records, but the Inventory is not open for reuse, so it is not used here.

      Tags are inconsistent. "Landmark" and "natural monument" mean different things to different mappers, {{counts.extract_girth_bulk|int}} girth tags in my extract are survey defaults repeated across whole streets, and {{counts.extract_girth_tagged|int}} trees have any girth at all. My credibility rules remove the worst values but cannot check a measurement.

      Churchyards and forests are approximations. A tree within {{church.m|int}} m of the centre of a church is usually in or beside its churchyard, but not always. Royal forest circles round a single Wikidata point are a crude stand-in for forest boundaries that changed over centuries.

      Ages are estimates. Mitchell's rule is a rule of thumb. Woodland trees grow more slowly, young trees faster, and ancient trees much more slowly. Wikipedia's ages for famous trees are themselves disputed ranges.

      How I did this

      Trees. OpenStreetMap natural=tree nodes inside the United Kingdom that have a name, a denotation, an age, a heritage, a Wikidata or a circumference tag, retrieved through the QLever endpoint on {{retrieved|raw}}: {{counts.osm_trees_extract|int}} trees. A tree is notable if it has a name (other than a fruit variety in an orchard), a denotation of landmark, natural monument, ancient or veteran, an age tag of veteran or a number of years of 100 or more, a Wikidata link, a heritage tag, or a credible girth of 3 m or more. It has a sign of great age if its credible girth is 3 m or more, it is tagged ancient or veteran or with an age, or it is a named or identified tree tagged as a natural monument. Species groups come from the species, taxon and genus tags, or from the name.

      Places. Country, region and county come from the nearest OS Open Names populated place; trees on the island of Ireland are in Northern Ireland. Places of worship: {{counts.worship|int}} Christian places of worship in OpenStreetMap, located at their centre point; Anglican, Church in Wales, Scottish Episcopal and Catholic denominations are grouped as the older churches. Royal forests: Wikidata items that are instances of royal forest, with a coordinate. Deer parks: OpenStreetMap areas named "deer park" or tagged as a historic deer park, of 0.05 km² or more.

      Famous trees. Eight ancient trees with English Wikipedia articles, with the girths and ages those articles state. Wikidata remarkable and heritage trees were checked against OpenStreetMap: found if an OSM tree carries their Wikidata link or lies within 150 m.
